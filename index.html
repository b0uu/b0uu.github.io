Attendance and Applications for Elite Colleges

Across 139 colleges in the United States, children from richer families attend at a far higher rate in comparison to those less fortunate.

The association between parental income and college attendance at selective U.S. colleges

Purpose:

We wanted to analyze how access to certain colleges in the United States can vary significantly depending on a student's economic background. Students from wealthier families are often represented in these institutions at much higher rates than students from lower income families, which leads to the question of why this occurs. For this project we wanted to specifically learn & display more about how parental income relates to college application and attendance patterns, and to what extent these relationships are different between institutions.

We used data from Opportunity Insights, with research from Raj Chetty, David Deming, and John Friedman. The dataset we used covers 139 colleges in the U.S., with parental income split into 14 different groups. We mainly focused on three measures: relative attendance, relative applications, and relative attendance given that the student has applied. These measures allow us to look at the differences in attendance while also considering the factor of the application process.

Our ultimate goal is to make these relationships accessible to explore by allowing user to interact with visualizations that compare colleges and income groups seamlessly.

Visualization:

We used a line chart and a scatterplot to visualize the data together. The line chart displays how relative attendance and application measures can vary depending on the parental income group. Each line represents a specific college, and income groups are arranged from low to high on the x-axis. The y-axis represents the relative rate on a log scale, which accounts for the differences in magnitude across institutions. Since income groups have a natural ordering (low to high), we naturally decided to go with a line chart such that change is easily visualized by audiences.

Displaying a large amount of colleges at the same time is one limitation of this line chart approach, since there's a lot of overlapping that makes it difficult to trace. In order to help with this, we have an additional scatterplot that compares the bottom 20% and the top 1% of the parental income distribution for each college.

In the scatterplot, each point represents a college, and the bottom 20% is on the x-axis while the top 1% is on the y-axis. There's a diagonal reference line that indicates where both groups have equal relative values. Any college that lies above the reference diagonal line has higher relative rates for the top 1% than the bottom 20%.

We considered other approaches, such as displaying each college separately, but we decided that we wanted to opt for a visualization that makes it easy to compare many institutions in parallel while preserving the entire distribution across income groups.

Interaction:

We implemented various interactions that make it intuitive for users to explore the data without being overwhelmed.

Users can access a dropdown menu that allows them to switch between relative attendance, relative applications, and relative attendance given student applying. This allows users to look at whether patterns change depending on the three measures instead of focusing on overall attendance without nuance. We also added filters for public and private institutions as well as college tiers, to allow for more filtering based on college type.

We also implemented brushing and linking between the line chart and scatterplot. Users are able to select a region of the scatterplot, and the selected colleges are highlighted in the line chart, making it seamless to identify trends between income groups. This is especially helpful due to the line chart being more difficult to read with every single datapoint displayed at the same time.

We've also added tooltips that display the college's name, classification, and relative values whenever the cursor hovers over. If clicked, the college continues to be highlighted, with reset returning the view back to default.
